1. CORE BELIEFS ABOUT SELLING

These are operating principles — not scripts. Internalize these before touching tactics.

People want to buy. Help their logical brain justify the decision they already want to make. Emotional buyers decide, then use logic to confirm. You must satisfy both.
Selling happens before you ask. Closing happens after. You're selling the entire conversation. The moment you name the price, you're closing.
It's easier to handle obstacles than objections. Before you ask for the sale, everything is an obstacle — easier to address. Once you've asked, it's an objection — harder.
Expect no. Plan for no. No is not failure — it's the job. If they were going to say yes automatically, you're not necessary.
If you didn't get a gasp from the price, you didn't go high enough. Train yourself (and your team) to be comfortable with the gasp. The gasp is good.
Selling properly is the first step in coaching. How you sell sets the frame for the entire relationship. Use logic in the sale = logical client.
Selling is helping prospects make decisions for themselves. You're not manipulating — you're giving people power. The goal is not "buy from me." The goal is "decide." You can win every sale if you change what winning means.
Vanish. Magnify them. The more you disappear in the conversation and make it about them, the better your close rate. They don't care about you. They care about them.
Childlike curiosity when they object. Not defensiveness. Not argument. "Huh. That's interesting. Tell me more about that."
Selling is a transference of belief over a bridge of trust. Two requirements: you must BELIEVE (fully, visibly), and they must TRUST you. Cold streaks happen when belief breaks down — not when sales skills disappear.
The person who cares most about the prospect wins the sale. If you care more about their outcome than they do, you will close. If you only care about the commission, they'll smell it.
Report every sales call. Review hot streaks — what did you say? How did you pause? When did you ask? This is how you replicate peak performance and train new reps.
2. THE C.L.O.S.E.R. FRAMEWORK

C — Clarify why they're there Get them to articulate their own pain, in their own words. Never tell them what their problem is before they've said it themselves. "What brought you here today? What's going on?"

L — Label their problem Restate it in a way that demonstrates deep understanding. Make them feel heard. "So what I'm hearing is [restatement] — is that right?"

O — Overview past experiences What have they tried before? What worked? What didn't? What did they lose (money, time, hope)? This builds stakes and establishes that they've already been paying a cost.

S — Sell them the vacation (not the plane ride) Present the dream outcome in vivid, specific language. Not "our program." Their life after. "Imagine six weeks from now, you've lost 20 pounds, your energy is through the roof, and you're doing the thing you've been putting off for two years..."

E — Explain away their concerns Use the 5-bucket objection framework. Don't wait for them to object — address concerns proactively based on what you've learned in C, L, and O.

R — Reinforce the decision After they say yes: validate, excite, and future-pace. Tell them exactly what happens next. Reduce buyer's remorse before it starts. "You made a great call. Here's what we're doing..."

3. OBSTACLES VS. OBJECTIONS

Critical distinction:

Obstacle = happens BEFORE you ask for the sale "I'm just looking to get some information first." "How do you compare to [competitor]?" "I'm not really sure if I'm ready." → Easier to handle. No stake has been declared. You're still in exploratory mode.

Objection = happens AFTER you ask for the sale "I can't afford it right now." "I need to think about it." "I have to talk to my spouse." → Harder. The sale has been solicited. Stakes are explicit.

Rule: Handle every obstacle before asking for the sale. Ask clarifying questions. Reveal the real concern. Move past it before you're in the red zone.

Obstacle example: "I just wanted to get more information first." → "Of course. Out of curiosity — do you go to a lot of different programs to gather information, or is there a specific problem you're trying to solve?" → They reveal the real issue. Now you can address it before asking.

4. THE THREE DISTORTIONS OF REALITY

All objections are distortions — irrational beliefs, not rational analysis. From Dr. Albert Ellis:

Distortion 1: Circumstances "I must get what I want when I want it. I must not get what I don't want. If I don't get what I want, I can't stand it." → Manifests as: time objections, money objections, fit objections

Distortion 2: Others "Others must treat me fairly. If they don't, they deserve to be condemned." → Manifests as: authority objections ("I need my spouse's approval"), trust concerns

Distortion 3: Self "I must do well, or I am no good." → Manifests as: avoidance, stalling, "I need to think about it"

The onion of blame: Prospects peel from outside to inside.

Circumstances (outermost): time, money, external factors
Others: spouse, boss, partner
Self (innermost): "I'm afraid I won't follow through"

When they blame circumstances, you haven't gotten to the real reason yet. Keep peeling.

5. THE FIVE OBJECTION BUCKETS
Bucket 1: TIME

"I'm really busy right now." / "This isn't a good time." / "When I have more time, I will."

Macro (busy season): "Do you think you're going to be busy again in the future? Then doesn't it make sense to learn how to do this when you're busy? If you can only do it in perfect circumstances, you'll fall off every time life gets busy — and that's exactly when you'd need the most support."

Micro (no time in the day): "I used to say the same thing. My wife pulled out my phone and showed me 22 hours on social media that week. Turns out I had plenty of time. The program isn't about adding more to your plate — it's about removing the 90% of stuff that's making you feel overwhelmed."

When/Then fallacy: "When I have more time, I'll do it." / "When I have more money, I'll invest." → "That's called the when/then fallacy. It's like saying 'I'll start saving money once I'm rich.' The thing you're waiting for is the thing we're here to create. Waiting for perfect conditions that the program is designed to give you makes no sense."

Bucket 2: MONEY / PRICE

"I can't afford it." / "It's too expensive." / "That's a lot of money."

Why a lot is good: "If this is a lot of money for you, that's exactly why you're going to succeed. You're all in. The people who pay the most pay the most attention. They take it seriously. I'm more excited about working with you because I know you'll show up."

Relative value: "If this does what I've told you it will do — if it [dream outcome] — is the price worth it?" (They say yes.) "Then the price isn't the issue. The issue is whether you believe it'll work. So let's talk about that."

What's money for anyway: "You're going to spend this money either way over the next 12 months. The question is whether you spend it on things that don't move you forward, or invest it in compressing time. You can learn these lessons in 12 weeks or 12 years. Which would you prefer?"

Resourcefulness: "Have you ever had an unexpected tax bill or emergency come up and somehow figured it out?" (Yes.) "Interesting. So you're resourceful when the stakes are for other people. What if the stakes were for you?" Every self-made person started with nothing. The difference is resourcefulness, not resources.

Bucket 3: FIT

"I'm not sure this is right for me." / "I don't think it'll work for my situation." / "I don't want to do [specific thing in the program]."

New identity, new priorities: "The version of you that wants the result has different priorities than the version of you standing here right now. Your current actions are producing your current results. To get different results, you have to do different things. The question is: how much more pain do you need before you're ready to do something different?"

Change the change: "You've been doing what you've been doing and getting what you've been getting. The program isn't going to give you more of the same. It's going to change the inputs — which is the only way to change the outputs. Is the pain of staying the same greater or less than the pain of making a change?"

Hypothetical close: "If this program were perfect for you — exactly what you needed — would you do it?" → If yes: "What would make it perfect? What's the gap between perfect and what I've described?" → If no: "Then something deeper is going on. What's the real concern?"

Proof close: "How many people would you need to see succeed in your exact situation before it would be more unreasonable NOT to believe than to believe? Is it 10? 50? Here are [number]. Tell me when to stop."

Bucket 4: AUTHORITY ("I have to talk to someone else")

"I need to talk to my spouse." / "I need to run this by my business partner."

If you can't get them in the room: Isolate the objection and bypass the absent party. "What specifically do you think they would be concerned about?" → They name it. You overcome that specific concern. The partner isn't really the issue.

The support vs. permission reframe: "Are they not supportive of you improving, or is this about needing permission?" "Do they approve of your current struggle? No. Then why would they disapprove of something designed to fix what they already don't approve of?" "If the roles were reversed and they needed this to achieve their dreams, would you support them? Then extend the same to yourself." "You're asking for permission to invest in yourself. Instead, tell them: 'I'm not asking permission. I'm asking for your support. I'm going to do this because I'm tired of [current situation], and I want to stop complaining about it and actually fix it.'"

Bucket 5: AVOIDANCE / STALL ("I need to think about it")

This is the deepest layer. You've peeled past circumstances, others, and now you're at the self — the real fear.

Past frame: "How long have you been thinking about this? Has 'I'll think about it' served you well up until now? That pattern is part of why you're here."

Sunk cost reframe: "You've already invested [time/money/effort] getting here. You're 6 inches from gold. Don't let a previous bad decision stop a good one. That burns you twice — once from the bad decision and once from letting it block the good one."

Present frame (rocking chair): "Let's walk through what 'thinking about it' looks like. You get in your car. You pick up the kids. You make dinner. Three days from now you put on those jeans that don't fit. You'll have made the decision to keep doing what you're doing. Let's decide now."

Information fallacy: "You don't need more time to decide. You need more information. I'm the only source of that information. I'm right here. What questions do you still have? Let's answer them and make a decision together."

Three questions close:

"Do you believe this will help you get [dream outcome]?" → Yes
"Do you trust me to deliver what I've said I'll deliver?" → Yes
"Do you believe it'll work specifically for you?" → If yes: proceed. If no: "Tell me why not." → They have to defend their skepticism with specifics → you address those.

Options close: "There are three possible outcomes here. One: you join, and it works. Two: you join, do the work, and it doesn't work — and we make it right because of our guarantee. Three: you don't join, and nothing changes. Only one of those options has a guaranteed bad outcome. Which risk-free option would you like?"

The reason = the reason TO do it: "The very reason you're giving me for not doing this is the reason you should. You can't afford it? That's why you need more income. You don't have time? That's why you need a more efficient system. You're not sure you can follow through? That's exactly what the accountability is for."

6. THE CLOSING SEQUENCE
After Asking for the Sale
Ask for the sale clearly. No ambiguity.
Shut up. Let them respond.
If they say yes → reveal bonuses, reinforce (the R in CLOSER)
If they raise an objection → identify the bucket, use the framework, re-ask once
If they raise a second objection → different bucket or same bucket deeper. Address and re-ask.
If still no → present a downsell. Change HOW they pay before changing WHAT they get.
If still no → offer trial with penalty or feature reduction. Get SOMETHING going.
The Three-Question Close (for avoidance)

After all objections are handled, ask directly:

"Do you believe this will get you the result?"
"Do you trust me to do what I said?"
"Do you believe it'll work for you?" If all yes: "Then the only question is how we get started. Do you have access to [amount]?" If they can find $10K for a tax bill, they can find it for this.
7. SALES TEAM DIAGNOSTICS

The close rate analysis:

40%+ close rate: volume problem (not enough leads)
35-40%: in the zone
25-35%: messaging or targeting problem
Below 25%: offer problem, or wrong salespeople, or both

How to identify a great sales director (interview question): "How does what you do make the company money?" Bad answer: "I'll drive sales." Good answer: "I'll increase conversion percentage across all leads from X% to Y% by tracking [9 specific metrics], by restructuring the follow-up cadence, and by implementing a qualification framework at top of funnel that removes unqualified leads before they hit our closers."

The quantity and quality of metrics test: How many metrics can they name for their function? How clearly do they tie each to revenue? One or two metrics = they don't understand the job. Seven to ten with clear reasoning = hire.

The team problem is almost always a standards problem: "Stupid rules mean you hired stupid people." If you need to tell adults not to watch Netflix during customer calls, you hired the wrong adults. Raise the bar on who gets in the door.

Conviction as a team problem: Track close rate by rep. When a rep's rate drops suddenly, the problem is usually not skill — it's belief. Something happened that shook their conviction in the product. Find it and fix it. "You can have the same skills as yesterday and make no sales today because you stopped believing."

8. CONVICTION & BELIEF BUILDING

The most overlooked lever in sales performance. Belief transfers. Doubt transfers.

Team conviction rituals:

Start every sales day reviewing 3 client success stories
Have a "wins board" visible in the workspace
Regular product deep-dives — team must experience what they sell
"Why I believe" conversations — reps articulate why the product works in their own words

The hot streak framework: Record every call. When a rep is on a hot streak, review those recordings specifically:

What tone did they use?
When did they pause?
How did they handle the first no?
What jokes worked? Use those recordings as training material and to help reps return to peak state.

The missionary vs. mercenary test: Ask every rep: "What's the best success story you've seen from a client?" If they can't name one vividly and with emotion — they're mercenaries. They'll fail under pressure because they don't actually believe in what they're selling.
