1. THE VALUE EQUATION

The formula:

Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)

Two variables to INCREASE (numerator):

Dream Outcome
Perceived Likelihood of Achievement

Two variables to DECREASE (denominator):

Time Delay
Effort & Sacrifice

Because it's division, reducing the denominator toward zero drives value toward infinity.

Dream Outcome

Not what you sell — what they experience at the destination. "Nobody wants a gym membership. They want to look good naked." "I wasn't selling a membership. I was selling the vacation."

Status is the meta-driver. The dream outcome that most directly increases a prospect's status will be valued most. Frame benefits through the eyes of others: "Your golf buddies' jaws will drop."

Perceived Likelihood of Achievement

People pay for certainty. A surgeon with 10,000 surgeries commands 10x the rate of one with 10. Increase this through: social proof, case studies, credentials, guarantees, specific methodology.

The power formula: "Provide proof so overwhelming that it's more unreasonable not to believe than to believe."

Time Delay

The gap between purchase and result. Two components:

Long-term outcome: what they bought (the dream)
Short-term experience: fast wins that prove they're on the right path

"The only thing that beats 'free' is 'fast.'" FedEx vs. USPS. Spotify vs. free downloads. Always build in immediate wins. In a gym business: pair them with a buddy and give aggressive early diet for visible results in week one.

Effort & Sacrifice

The real cost beyond price — physical, emotional, social, time. Liposuction vs. personal training: same outcome (weight loss), massively different perceived value. Liposuction costs $25K, takes an afternoon, done-for-you. Personal training costs a fraction but requires months of discipline.

"Done-for-you services are almost always more expensive than do-it-yourself because the person doesn't have to supply the effort and sacrifice."

The supplements industry ($123B) is 2x the size of the health club industry ($62B). Same dream. One requires swallowing a pill. The other requires showing up every day.

Where Most Operators Get It Wrong

Beginners focus on the numerator — bigger claims about dream outcomes. The best companies in the world focus on the denominator: faster, easier, more certain. Amazon is a denominator company. The biggest increase in London Underground satisfaction didn't come from faster trains — it came from a dotted map showing when the next train arrived. Psychological speed costs nothing.

2. THE 4 BUSINESS SHAPES

Every business is one of four shapes. Each has a growth curve and a core hairy problem. When a founder thinks something is "wrong" with their business, it's usually a feature of the model — not a bug.

Shape 1: E-Commerce (Physical Products)

Growth curve: Fast ramp, then hard stops at breakpoints (supply chain, distribution, cash) Pros: Scales fast in revenue, high consumer willingness to pay, less key-man risk Cons: Capital intensive, inventory risk, supply chain dependencies, margin squeeze, easy to copy

Core hairy problems:

Cash flow: forecast inventory cycles 12+ weeks out
Distribution: ads, retail partners, affiliates — live and die here
Supply chain: redundant manufacturers, redundant 3PLs
Brand: only moat that can't be copied

The perfect e-commerce company: Owns manufacturing (or has trade secret ingredients), 70/30 brand/performance marketing split, retail + online + email, exceptional backend email marketing, product so good word-of-mouth is a real growth lever.

Shape 2: Service Business (78% of US businesses)

Growth curve: Slow and steady, nearly linear Pros: Lowest risk, always profitable (can cut to just yourself), high cash flow, sticky clients Cons: People-heavy at every stage, talent is the bottleneck, hard to scale past 2-3x/year

Core hairy problems:

Talent: finding, onboarding, and training exceptional people
Founder dependency: you're usually the best technician — must productize your knowledge
Systematization: without it, every delivery is bespoke and unscalable

How to win: Niche down to one avatar, 1-3 core deliverables, systematize delivery, build the best recruiting/training machine in your industry. Raise prices until you're supply-constrained. Your price level tells you how well you've solved the talent problem.

The north star metric: Price per engagement. If you're supply-constrained, raise prices. Keep raising until demand is the constraint — rare but achievable.

Shape 3: Education / Info / Consulting

Growth curve: Fastest initial ramp of all four, then hard plateau Pros: Fast initial cash, low startup cost, high margins, powerful personal brand flywheel Cons: Retention near zero (people graduate), you create your own competition, growth requires constant marketing, reputational risk if ops aren't exceptional

Core hairy problems:

Retention in a fundamentally unsticky model
You teach people who then compete with you
Scaling delivery without diluting quality (shot glass of good milk vs. glass of bad milk)

How to win:

Split one-time education (charge high, no recurrence) from consumable components (charge low, recurring) — "Big head, long tail"
Add stickiness: community, consumable resources, continued education credits, group buying power
Real-world proof is the brand — don't start teaching until you've done the thing at scale

The milk analogy: If you have the world's best milk (skill), you can give people a shot glass of exceptional milk OR a full glass of diluted milk. Most education businesses choose the full glass. Smart operators give the shot glass and charge accordingly.

Shape 4: SaaS / Software

Growth curve: Slowest start (long pre-revenue build), then potential to compound infinitely Pros: Infinite scale, highest gross margins, sticky revenue, massive enterprise value Cons: Expensive to build, long pre-revenue runway, VCs or cash required, slow to start

Core hairy problems:

Survive to product-market fit (boulder pushing uphill)
Revenue retention (logo churn vs. revenue retention — aim for >100% net revenue retention)
Viral coefficient: one user must bring 1.1+ users or paid ads become unsustainable at scale

How to win:

Obsess over the product — remove all friction between user and their desired outcome
Measure: activation, time-to-value, 30/60/90-day retention, NPS during value moments
Build viral loops into the product from day one
Quality > quantity for engineering talent (10x engineers exist and are worth it)
"The way to make exceptional software: find what outcome everyone wants, remove all friction between where they are and getting that outcome." — Steve Jobs did this with the iPhone.
3. PRICING PHILOSOPHY

Core principle: Charge as much as humanly possible without exceeding the value delivered. Never compete on price. You can only go so low, but you can go infinitely high on value.

The Virtuous Cycle: Charge more → more emotional investment → clients take action → better results → better testimonials → more referrals → can invest more in delivery → charge even more

The Vicious Cycle (avoid): Cut price → margin drops → can't fulfill → quality drops → complaints → cut more → death spiral

Close rate as a pricing diagnostic:

80%+ close rate → underpriced 3-4x, raise immediately
60-80% → underpriced 2-3x
35-40% → sweet spot
Below 30% → probably a sales process or targeting problem, not a price problem

Never discount. Add bonuses instead. Discounting signals weakness and trains customers that prices are negotiable. Bonuses signal strength and create goodwill.

The premium positioning rule: Sell to people who have money. Rich people pay for:

Time savings above all else
Certainty of outcome
Zero effort required of them

The niche premium: Generic weight loss course = $19. Weight loss for shift nurses = $1,997. Same core content. Different specificity. The more specific your avatar and problem, the higher you can charge — up to 100x more.

4. BRAND AS COMPOUND INTEREST

Direct response = paycheck (write a check, get money, stop spending, stop making). Brand = retirement account (invest for years, then compound interest takes over and outpaces anything direct response can achieve).

How brand is built (three levers):

What you say/show (advertising, content) — fully controllable
What other people say (word of mouth, reviews) — influenced by quality of experience
What customers experience when they use the product — the heaviest weight of the three

Brand is measured by how much above commodity price you can charge. Nike shirts vs. generic. If you sell 1M units and can charge $3 above market because of brand = $3M annual brand premium.

The brand flywheel: Make big promise → deliver on it → word of mouth → more customers → better margin → invest in delivery → make bigger promise. Repeat until the brand is so strong that customers don't shop around.

Content as brand building: Every piece of free content that delivers value approximates the trust that comes from a personal relationship. "If you spend 5 hours with someone, they trust you as much as someone who has known you for a year on Instagram."

5. TALENT PHILOSOPHY

Core belief: The best talent you've ever hired is your worst future hire. Your best talent is always in the future.

The arbitrage: An A-player costs 50% more than a B-player and delivers 5-10x the output. Five B-players at $100K = $500K. One A++ at $170K = 10x the output. The math is obvious.

Raising the bar rule (Amazon's standard): Every new hire should raise the average bar of the team they're joining. Sixes hire fives, fives hire fours. Actively fight this tendency.

Stupid rules = low standards. If you have rules like "show up on time" or "don't watch Netflix during calls," your problem isn't the employees — it's that you hired people who need those rules. Raise the bar; eliminate the rules.

The Peter Principle trap: People advance to their point of incompetency. A great salesperson becomes a bad sales manager. A great individual contributor becomes a bad VP. Promote carefully. Be willing to restructure rather than let someone fail in a role they weren't built for.

How to identify great talent: Ask how they make the business money. A great sales director doesn't say "I'll drive sales" — they say "I'll increase conversion percentage across all leads from the current X% to Y%, here's the 9 metrics I'll track to get there, here's how each one ties to revenue." Can they quantify their own output?

6. THE CONSTRAINT PRINCIPLE

Every business has exactly one binding constraint at any given time. Everything else is noise until that constraint is solved. This is the Theory of Constraints applied to business.

"Find the constraint. Fix the constraint. Let it grow."

The bridge analogy: A bridge can handle 10,000 pounds except for one loose brick that limits it to 1,000. You can reinforce everything else on that bridge — doesn't matter. Fix the one loose brick. Then find the next one.

How to find the constraint: Ask: "Why can't we 10x [the key metric] right now?" Every answer is either the real constraint or points to it.

Common constraints by stage:

0 → $1M: offer and sales (most people can't close)
$1M → $10M: operations and talent (can't hire fast enough)
$10M → $100M: systems and culture (founder can't let go)
$100M+: capital allocation and leadership depth

The misallocation trap: Most entrepreneurs solve the problems they're best at or enjoy most — not the actual constraint. Marketing founders fix marketing when sales is broken. Product founders perfect the product when distribution is the problem. The business is only as strong as its weakest link.

7. SELL TO RICH PEOPLE FIRST

Tesla's model: Roadster ($250K) → Model S ($100K) → Model X ($80K) → Model 3 ($40K). Never started at the bottom. Built infrastructure, brand, and capital from the top down.

Why this matters:

Rich people pay for time, certainty, and zero effort — charge premiums for these
High margin means you can over-deliver without going broke
No need for massive infrastructure/volume to be profitable
They don't nickel-and-dime; they evaluate on outcome

Sell to the masses only when:

You've built the infrastructure to handle volume (like Amazon)
You have the capital to sustain low-margin, high-volume operations
You've decided from day one to win on efficiency (Walmart, Amazon)

The $99/month customer vs. $5,000/month customer: The $99 customer expects 10% of their paycheck worth of results and will hold you to it. The $5,000 customer, if they get one good insight per year, sends the wire without asking.

8. BETTER BEFORE BIGGER

Growth is a lagging indicator. The inputs that create growth are the leading indicators. Trying to grow before the product/service is exceptional just means more people learn it's bad.

Chick-fil-A vs. Boston Market: Boston Market grew faster, took on debt, diluted talent, went bankrupt. Chick-fil-A focused on quality, grew responsibly, and won in the long run. Missionaries beat mercenaries. If money is the goal, there are too many paths — you get distracted. If product excellence is the goal, everything else becomes clear.

The pelaton standard: "What would we have to do to make this so good that the next person they talk to, it's the first thing they bring up?" That's the quality threshold.

100 golden BBs, not one silver bullet. Excellence comes from stacking 100 small improvements, each of which compounds. Not from finding one hack. Coats of paint — you make money in the edit, in the distillation, not in the first draft.

9. BUSINESS MATH THAT MATTERS

The only two levers: Make customers worth more (LTV), or make them cost less to acquire (CAC). Every business activity must map to one of these two. If it doesn't, stop doing it.

LTGP:CAC benchmarks:

Fully automated (SaaS, no human touch): 3:1
Some human element: 6:1
Multiple human touchpoints: 9:1
Manual business, cold market: 20:1-30:1
Gym Launch year one: 100:1 ($100K spent, $10M made)

Payback period target: Recoup customer acquisition cost within 30 days. Front-load cash through upfront offers, payment in full incentives, pre-selling.

The "why can't you 10x ad spend" diagnostic:

Row AS drops when you scale → ads are bad
Can't handle call volume → hiring/training problem
Can't deliver at scale → systematization problem
Already at market saturation → distribution or offer problem Each answer tells you exactly what to fix.

Revenue retention (SaaS/subscription):

60%+ logo retention by year-end: decent product

100% net revenue retention: exceptional — expansion revenue exceeds churn

This is what separates a good business from an incredibly valuable one

Three ways to grow:

More customers (volume)
Higher average order value (price × attach rate)
More purchases per customer (LTV × frequency)

Stack all three and growth compounds non-linearly.
